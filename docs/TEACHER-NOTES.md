# Teacher notes

How the atlas fits the Unit 1 lesson sequence, and what to do with it.

---

## The one-sentence version

The atlas turns the book's five flat maps into layers students can interrogate, and puts all 53 primary sources on the same spatial canvas so students can see that a source has a *place* as well as a date.

---

## Where it fits the standards

| Strand | Weight | How the atlas serves it |
|---|---|---|
| **Questioning, Planning & Evaluating Sources (QP)** | 30% | Every source states its rights, holder, and locational certainty, and links to the archive record. The `correction` notes put two accounts of the same object side by side and ask students which to trust. |
| **Develop Knowledge & Understanding (KU)** | 40% | *History* — the time slider makes change over time literal. *Geography* — the graticule toggle shows cane's latitude constraint. *Economics* — the spherical trade layer is the production-and-distribution system in one image. *Civics* — the resistance and abolition layer is where rights get claimed. |
| **Informed Communication with Action (ICA)** | 30% | Students argue from the map: pick a layer, make a claim, cite the pins that support it. |

## Three ready-to-run activities

### 1. The certainty audit (QP, 20 min)

Open **Sources**. Give each pair five sources. For each one they record: the certainty label, who holds it, and whether the book's caption matches the archive's own title.

Then the discussion question: *the map tells you when it isn't sure. Why would a historian build that in, and what would you lose if it didn't?*

Two sources make this land hardest:

- **St Kitts, 1901** — the book says "Two Sugar Workers"; the Library of Congress says *"Two pretty girls I met in a cane field."* Which title is doing work, and for whom?
- **The elephant plough** — commonly credited to the *Illustrated London News*, actually from *Gleason's Pictorial* of Boston, and the book itself asks whether elephants really ploughed this way at all.

### 2. Same place, different century (KU, 25 min)

Antigua 1823 (Clark, Plate IV — *Cutting the cane*) beside Ponce, Puerto Rico 1938 (Rosskam). A painting for planters and a New Deal photograph, 115 years apart.

Students list what changed and what didn't. The list of what didn't is almost always longer, and that is the lesson.

### 3. Follow the thread (ICA, 40 min)

Turn on **The spherical trade**. Each group takes one node — Potosí, Manila, an Indian weaving town, Newport, Luanda, London — and writes 150 words tracing how their place connects to a spoonful of sugar in an English cup.

Then they present in a circle, in order, so the class builds the globe out loud. The point of the book's argument on p. 37 is that no single group can finish the loop alone.

---

### 4. The China–Cuba Connection (QP + KU, 40 min)

Five steps in Part Four carry a **China–Cuba Connection** button. Each opens the *Cuba Commission Report* of 1876 — the Qing government's investigation into the Chinese labourers shipped to Cuba, built on 1,176 depositions taken on the island in seven weeks of 1874.

Every panel runs the same four moves, and it is worth naming them for students because it is the shape of historical argument: **what the book says → what the Commission found → the workers' own words → now you compare.**

| Button on | The comparison it sets up |
|---|---|
| `A new system` | Gladstone writes to Calcutta in 1836; Cuba's first Chinese land in 1847. Same logic, nine years apart — but Cuba was still a slave colony. |
| `Crossing the black water` | The *kali pani* against the Pacific passage. The book gives twenty-seven weeks; the Report gives 16,000 dead out of 140,000. |
| `Slavery or freedom? The in-between` | The book asks the question. The men-market at Havana answers it — in the first person. |
| `Bechu writes to the newspapers` | One man's letters in 1896 against 1,176 depositions in 1874. Which was harder to dismiss? |
| `Sugar in paradise` | Hawaii's Chinese workers and Cuba's came from the same few counties in the Pearl River delta. |

**The best single discussion.** Open the `in-between` panel and put two lines beside each other on the board — the book's *"life was like being a prisoner on parole"* and Liu A-jui's *"I am treated exactly like a negro slave."* One is a historian's summary written in 2010; the other is a man describing his own life in 1874. Ask which is the better evidence, and then ask what each one can do that the other cannot. There is no clean answer, which is the point.

**Two things to be straight with students about.**

- The book does not use this source. It is here to test the book against, and the source panel says so in its `correction` field.
- The 1,176 depositions are not in the digitised volume — only the passages the commissioners chose to quote. So even this source has an editor standing between the students and the men who spoke. That is a ten-minute conversation on its own.

**A warning about the material.** The depositions describe suicide, mutilation, and men beaten to death, in plain language. The quotations in the panels were chosen to make the system legible rather than to shock, but they are not softened. Read the `in-between` panel yourself before running it with a class.

## Things students reliably notice (and what to do with them)

**"Why are the plantation dots all in a line?"** — Because cane needs the tropics. This is the moment geography stops being background and becomes the cause. Turn the graticule on and let them say it themselves.

**"Why is 1493 red?"** — The only red arc in the diffusion layer is Columbus carrying cane from Gomera. Ask what makes an event a hinge rather than just another date.

**"Why are two sources just empty pins?"** — Levni's sugar gardens and Testard's aloe wood are real, correctly identified, and not freely available online. Worth ten minutes on whose history gets digitised, and who has to pay a museum for access to their own past.

**"The book says Jamaica 1665 but the pin says check it."** — Good. Let them check it. The book is a secondary source too.

**"Why are the Chinese men's names written like that?"** — Because the Report romanised them in 1876, and the atlas keeps its spellings with the characters it printed alongside. Worth noting that many deponents also state they were shipped under someone else's name entirely — the Report has a whole page of it. A man could arrive in Cuba with another man's name on his contract, which is a hard fact about how much the paperwork was worth.

## Practical

- **No login, no account, no data collection.** It is a static website.
- **Works on a locked-down school network.** MapLibre and the basemap are served from the repository, not a CDN.
- **Phones work**, though the Clark plates deserve a bigger screen.
- **Projector use**: press <kbd>Esc</kbd> to close any panel; the Story steps advance by scrolling the left panel.
- **First load** is about 2 seconds; source images load only when opened.
