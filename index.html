Primary sources

Every source below is pinned to a place on the map. Click one to fly there and open it. Each carries a prompt: OPTIC for images, SCRAP for maps.

Read the map before you read the sources

Every pin is a record that survived — someone made it, and someone kept it. Nothing is pinned where a record was never made, or was made and thrown away. So where this map is empty is evidence too.

Colour the pins by
SCRAP — five questions for this map
S
Spatial–temporal. Where are the pins, and what years do they cover? There is no scale bar on this map, so make no claim about distance — say that out loud.
C
Conditions, connections, comparisons. Turn on Plantations in Explore and compare. Sugar was grown in one set of places. Was the record made in the same places?
R
Region. Draw a line around the part of the map that is crowded, and another around a part that is empty. What holds each one together?
A
Aura. The map looks full. Name that feeling first — then say what produced it. Fifty-three pins on a world map is not many.
P
Pattern and exceptions. The counts above are the pattern. Now find the exceptions: the two pins that carry no picture at all, and the one place whose entire record is two faces of the same object.

About this atlas

An interactive companion to Sugar Changed the World: A Story of Magic, Spice, Slavery, Freedom, and Science by Marc Aronson and Marina Budhos (Clarion Books, 2010).

What it re-creates

The book carries five maps. All five are rebuilt here as live layers rather than flat pictures — the spread of sugar, the areas where cane was grown, sugar crossing the Atlantic, sugar and Atlantic slavery, and Indian indentured workers 1835–1917. A sixth layer maps the book's "spherical trade" argument, which the book makes in prose but never draws.

How to read the pins

Each primary source states how confident its location is:

  • Located — the archive record names the place.
  • Approximate — the place is inferred from the record.
  • Symbolic — the object has no single place; it is pinned where it belongs in the argument.
  • Uncertain — the record does not say, and the pin is a best guess.

Some sources carry a correction. These are not errors in the map; they are places where the book's caption, the archive's title, or a common attribution disagree. They are shown on purpose.

Analysis routines

OPTIC (Overview, Parts, Title, Interrelationships, Conclusion) for photographs and images. SCRAP (Spatial–temporal; Conditions/Connections/Comparisons; Region; Aura/Association/Analog; Pattern or Exceptions) for maps. Both come from Jo, Crane, Hong & Huh, "GeoActivity Types in APHG: Analysis of Maps and Photos," The Geography Teacher 19(2), 2022.

The China–Cuba Connection

Five steps in Part Four carry a China–Cuba Connection button. These open a second primary source that the book does not use: the Cuba Commission Report (Shanghai: Imperial Maritime Customs Press, 1876), the Chinese government's investigation into the Chinese labourers shipped to Cuba between 1847 and 1874. Three commissioners spent seven weeks on the island in 1874 and took 1,176 depositions and 85 petitions carrying 1,665 signatures.

The book's Part Four follows Indian indenture to British Guiana. The Cuba Report is the same system running at the same time across a different ocean — with one difference the panels keep in front of students: Cuba was still a slave colony, so these men worked beside enslaved Africans rather than after them.

Each panel sets what the book says against what the Report found, quotes the workers directly, and ends with a comparison task. Every quotation was checked against the page image of the 1876 printing, not the OCR text layer, which reflows the printed columns and misattributes quotations. Page numbers cite the printed page of the Report.

One limit worth telling students. The digitised copy contains the commissioners' report and the Spanish colonial regulations, but the 1,176 depositions were bound as appendices and are not in it. What you can read are the passages the commissioners chose to quote — which is itself a source question.

Built with

MapLibre GL JS, and a basemap built from Natural Earth public-domain vectors served from this repository. No API keys, no tile bills, no tracking, no third-party map service. The whole site is static files.

Sources and rights

Images are web derivatives of a curated high-resolution archive. Most are public domain. Exceptions carried through from the source notes: British Library plates are CC0; the Brunias is CC0 (Yale Center for British Art); V&A images are capped at 2500px by the museum; Royal Museums Greenwich at 1280px; Wellcome items carry the Public Domain Mark. Each source panel names its holder and links out.

Two images in the book could not be obtained freely and appear as pins without pictures — the Levni sugar-garden folios at Topkapı, and the Testard aloe-wood miniature in St Petersburg. Neither is openly digitised.
